22 Types of Camera Shots and Angles in Photography and Film

Introduction

Camera shots and angles are the visual language of photography and filmmaking. Understanding them gives you the vocabulary to tell stories, evoke emotions, and guide viewer attention. From extreme close-ups to aerial perspectives, each shot type serves a distinct purpose.

This guide covers 22 essential camera shots and angles, what they communicate, and when to use them.

Shot Types by Framing

1. Extreme Wide Shot (EWS)

The subject is barely visible, dwarfed by the environment. Use for: Establishing location, conveying isolation or scale.

2. Wide Shot / Long Shot (WS/LS)

The full subject is visible with surrounding environment. Use for: Context, action scenes, showing relationships between subject and space.

3. Full Shot (FS)

Subject fills the frame from head to toe. Use for: Fashion, full-body portraits, character introductions.

4. Medium Wide Shot (MWS)

Subject from knees or waist up. Use for: Group shots, environmental portraits, scenes with multiple subjects.

5. Medium Shot (MS)

Subject from waist up. Use for: Dialogue, interviews, standard portraiture. The most natural, conversational framing.

6. Medium Close-Up (MCU)

Subject from chest or shoulders up. Use for: Emotional reactions, professional headshots, television-style framing.

7. Close-Up (CU)

Face fills the frame, from neck to just above the head. Use for: Intense emotion, intimacy, revealing character.

8. Extreme Close-Up (ECU)

A single feature—eyes, mouth, hands—fills the frame. Use for: Detail, tension, symbolic imagery.

9. Detail Shot

Similar to ECU, focusing on objects rather than body parts. Use for: Product photography, storytelling details, texture emphasis.

10. Two-Shot / Three-Shot

Two or three subjects in a single frame, usually in medium or wide framing. Use for: Conversations, relationships, group dynamics.

11. Over-the-Shoulder (OTS)

Shot from behind one subject, showing what they're looking at—often another person. Use for: Conversations, point-of-view context, creating depth.

12. Point of View (POV)

The camera becomes the character's eyes. Use for: Immersion, subjective experience, first-person storytelling.

Angles

13. Eye Level

Camera at the subject's eye height. Use for: Neutral, relatable, documentary-style imagery.

14. High Angle

Camera positioned above, looking down. Use for: Making subjects appear vulnerable, small, or overviewing a scene.

15. Low Angle

Camera positioned below, looking up. Use for: Making subjects appear powerful, heroic, or imposing.

16. Dutch Angle / Dutch Tilt

Camera tilted so the horizon is diagonal. Use for: Disorientation, tension, unease, dynamic energy.

17. Bird's Eye View / Overhead

Camera directly above, looking straight down. Use for: Patterns, graphic compositions, food and flat-lay photography.

18. Worm's Eye View

Extreme low angle, as if from ground level. Use for: Dramatic perspective, towering subjects, surreal imagery.

19. Shoulder Level

Camera at shoulder height, slightly below eye level. Use for: Slightly authoritative perspective without the drama of a low angle.

20. Hip Level

Camera at hip height. Use for: Candid street photography, child's-eye perspective, casual framing.

21. Knee Level

Camera at knee height. Use for: Dramatic low perspectives without going fully to ground level.

22. Ground Level

Camera resting on or just above the ground. Use for: Unique foregrounds, small subjects, dramatic upward perspectives.

Practical Tips

  • Mix shot types for visual variety—wide for context, close for emotion
  • Use angles deliberately; every angle communicates something
  • Dutch angles work best sparingly for intentional effect
  • Eye level is the default for natural, honest photography
  • Low angles add power; high angles add vulnerability
  • Practice one new shot type per shoot until it becomes instinctive

Conclusion

Mastering camera shots and angles expands your visual storytelling toolkit. Wide shots establish context, close-ups reveal emotion, and deliberate angles guide viewer interpretation. The more intentionally you choose your framing and perspective, the more powerful your images become.

FAQ

What's the most versatile shot type for portraits? The medium close-up (chest/shoulders up) is most versatile—flattering for most subjects, good for headshots and environmental portraits, and works in both studio and location settings.

When should I use Dutch angles? Sparingly and intentionally. Dutch angles communicate unease, tension, or dynamic energy. Overuse makes images feel amateurish rather than artistic.

What angle is most flattering for portraits? Slightly above eye level, with the subject tilting their chin slightly down and forward. This defines the jawline and minimizes double chins.

How do I decide between wide and close shots? Wide shots tell stories about place and context. Close shots tell stories about emotion and character. Most effective sequences alternate between both.

Do these shot types apply to smartphone photography? Absolutely. The principles of framing and angle are universal. Smartphone cameras handle most of these shots effectively, especially with additional lenses.
